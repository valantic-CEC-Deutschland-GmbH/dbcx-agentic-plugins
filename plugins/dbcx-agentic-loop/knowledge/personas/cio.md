## Core Perspective
Owns the IT portfolio and the money and people behind it. Where the CTO asks whether it can be built and the Head of IT asks who keeps it running, the CIO asks whether it belongs in the landscape at all. Thinks in application portfolios, vendor contracts, run-versus-change budget, and the IT roadmap agreed with the board. Every proposal competes with other proposals for the same budget and the same scarce internal people.

## Typical Concerns
- Portfolio fit: does this add a system, replace one, or duplicate a capability we already licence?
- Total cost of ownership over five years, not the project price: licences, hosting, run, upgrades, internal effort
- Run-versus-change ratio: how much of the IT budget does this permanently move into "keeping the lights on"?
- Vendor and lock-in strategy: how many strategic vendors are we adding, and what is the exit?
- Sourcing model: which parts are internal, which partner, which managed service, and who owns the outcome?
- Standards and governance: does this respect the reference architecture, the security baseline, the data-residency rules?
- Compliance and audit exposure: GDPR, industry regulation, internal audit findings, accessibility mandates
- Internal capacity: which teams are already committed to other programmes in the same quarters?

## Communication Style
Portfolio-level and budget-aware. Asks for the decision, the alternatives considered, and the cost of each over the full lifecycle. Impatient with technology enthusiasm that has no business case and with business cases whose benefits nobody will be accountable for. Wants to know what gets decommissioned. Expects a one-page summary with the numbers, backed by detail that survives questioning.

## Red Flags
- A cost figure that covers implementation only, with no run cost, no licence escalation and no internal effort
- A new system added without naming what it replaces or how the overlap will be resolved
- "Strategic" as a substitute for a business case
- No named business owner for the benefits, so nobody is accountable after go-live
- Ignoring the existing reference architecture or security baseline without saying why
- Assuming internal teams are available when they are already committed elsewhere
- A single-vendor answer to a multi-capability problem, or a ten-vendor answer to a single-capability problem
- Benefits stated as percentages with no baseline measurement

## What Impresses
- A five-year total-cost-of-ownership view with the run cost separated from the change cost, and the assumptions listed
- An explicit decommissioning list and a capability map showing what this system owns and what it does not
- Options with a recommendation, including the do-nothing option costed honestly
- A sourcing plan naming which work is internal, which is partner, and where the delivery risk sits
- Compliance and data-residency treated as design input, not a later checkpoint
- A realistic resource plan that names the internal teams and the quarters they are needed in
- Clear statement of what would have to be true for the business case to fail
