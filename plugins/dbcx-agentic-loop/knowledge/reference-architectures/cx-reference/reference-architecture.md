# CX / Commerce Reference Architecture

Everything in this file is REFERENCE GUIDANCE, not a finding. No row, table cell or bullet below is a statement about any particular client: it is the pattern that holds in a typical commerce and customer-experience landscape, and it must be confirmed against the client's own systems, interfaces, contracts, data ownership decisions and organisation before it is used. A deliverable must cite the client's own systems and interfaces by name, with the evidence that was actually seen (an interface list, a schema, a configuration file, a workshop statement and who made it) — never this file. Where the client's landscape contradicts a row here, the client's landscape is right and the row is the question that uncovered it. Use the file to generate questions and to structure a gap grid; do not paste it into a report.

## 1. Capability map

| capability | what it owns | typical system types | canonical SSOT | notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Commerce / transaction | Cart, checkout, payment authorisation handover, promotion evaluation at cart level, order capture, B2B buying rules (approval, budget, cost centre spend) | Commerce platform (monolithic suite or composable commerce engine), B2B commerce module, payment service provider | Commerce platform for the cart and the captured order; the PSP for the payment transaction | The cart is genuinely mastered here — it exists nowhere else. Everything else the shop shows is a copy. Distinguish "owns the cart" from "owns the order after capture" — different systems, often confused |
| Content | Editorial pages, campaign and landing pages, navigation, editorial media placement, localisation and translation of editorial copy, personalisation rules on content | CMS / DXP, headless CMS, page builder inside the commerce suite | CMS | Product marketing copy is a boundary case: descriptive, translatable, per-locale attributes belong to the PIM; free-form editorial storytelling belongs to the CMS. Decide per attribute, not per system |
| Customer data | Identity, credentials, profile, addresses, communication preferences, B2B account and contact relationships, segments | CRM, CIAM / IdP, CDP, ERP business partner | CRM or the business-partner record in the ERP for the account; CIAM for identity and credentials; CDP for derived segments | Split identity (who may log in) from profile (what we know) from account (who is billed and under which terms). Three different masters is normal and not a violation; the same attribute in three masters is |
| Product data | Structure and classification, descriptive and marketing attributes, media assignment, relations (accessory, successor, cross-sell), locale variants, channel-specific enrichment and publication state | PIM, DAM (for the asset itself), ERP material master, commerce catalogue | PIM for enriched product data; ERP material master for commercial and logistic attributes (unit of measure, tax class, weight, status, material number) | The commerce catalogue is a published projection of the PIM, not a place to edit. If editors maintain product attributes in the shop backoffice, the shop has become a second PIM |
| Price | List prices, price lists per customer or channel, scales and tiers, contract prices, discount and condition logic, tax determination | ERP (condition technique), dedicated pricing engine, CPQ, commerce platform price rows | ERP or the pricing engine | Distinguish the price the customer is entitled to (ERP / pricing engine) from the promotion evaluated in the cart (commerce). Both may apply; only one may master the condition record |
| Inventory | Physical and available-to-promise stock per location, allocations, reservations, incoming stock and dates | ERP, WMS, inventory / availability service, OMS | ERP or WMS | The shop holds a cache with an explicit staleness budget and a rule for what happens when the cache is wrong at checkout. A shop that stores authoritative stock has taken on a warehouse's accountability |
| Order | Order lifecycle after capture: validation, sourcing and allocation, splitting, fulfilment orchestration, invoicing, returns and credits | ERP (sales order), OMS, commerce order-management module | OMS where one exists, otherwise the ERP | The shop is the order-capture channel. Once the order is handed over, the shop holds a status projection for display. Two systems progressing the same order lifecycle is the most expensive duplicate-mastership case |
| Service | Cases and tickets, service history, returns and complaints handling, entitlement and warranty, installed base | CRM / service cloud, ticketing system, field-service system | CRM / service system | Self-service in the shop is a UI over the service system. Cases created only in the shop and never mirrored are invisible to the agents who must answer them |
| Marketing | Campaigns, journeys, consent-scoped outbound messaging, audiences and segments, offer eligibility | Marketing automation, CDP, campaign management, consent management platform | Marketing automation for the campaign; CDP for the audience; CMP for the consent state | Consent is not marketing data — it is a legal record with its own master and its own audit trail. Treat it as a separate domain |
| Analytics | Behavioural events, funnels, attribution, business KPIs, reporting models, experiment results | Web / product analytics, CDP, data warehouse or lakehouse, BI | Warehouse / lakehouse for reported figures; the event collector for raw behaviour | Analytics is downstream of everything and masters nothing operational. When a process reads a KPI back out of the warehouse to make a transactional decision, the warehouse has become an operational dependency |
| Integration | Interface contracts, routing, transformation and mapping, protocol mediation, retries and error handling, message store and replay, monitoring of flows | iPaaS / integration platform, ESB, API gateway, event broker / streaming platform | The integration platform owns the contract and the mapping — it must not own a domain's data | An integration layer that holds state a business process depends on has become an undeclared application. Distinguish routing and mapping (legitimate) from business rules and derived data (not) |

## 2. SSOT responsibilities per data domain

| data domain | canonical master | writers | readers | classic violation | consequence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Product master (structure, enrichment, media assignment, relations) | PIM, with the ERP as the source of commercial and logistic attributes | PIM editors; the ERP for its own attribute set via interface; supplier feeds via PIM import | Commerce platform, search index, CMS, marketplaces, print and feed exports, service portal | Product attributes maintained directly in the shop backoffice "because it was quicker", so the PIM export overwrites them or is switched off for those fields | Two divergent product truths; the shop becomes uncontrolled and unmigratable; every new channel needs its own enrichment; a platform move must reconstruct the master from the shop database |
| Price and conditions | ERP condition records, or a dedicated pricing engine where the logic exceeds the ERP's condition technique | ERP pricing maintenance; contract negotiation processes; the pricing engine's rule authors | Commerce platform, CPQ, quoting, invoicing, sales portals, reporting | Customer-specific discounts re-implemented as shop promotions so they can be shown faster | The invoice and the shop disagree; sales loses trust in the channel; discount logic must be maintained twice and drifts on the first exception |
| Inventory and ATP | ERP or WMS; an availability service may aggregate but not originate | ERP / WMS goods movements; allocation and reservation processes | Commerce platform (cached), OMS, store systems, customer service | The shop stores the authoritative stock figure and decrements it itself, or the cache has no staleness budget and no oversell rule | Oversell and cancellations, or hidden safety buffers that suppress sellable stock; nobody can say which figure was right at the moment of the order |
| Customer and account master | CRM or CDP for the person and profile; the B2B account hierarchy is usually mastered in the ERP as the business partner, because billing, credit and terms hang off it | CRM / CDP for profile and segment; ERP for account, hierarchy, credit and terms; CIAM for credentials | Commerce platform, service, marketing, analytics, billing | Self-registration in the shop creates accounts and hierarchies that exist nowhere else, or the shop maintains a parallel customer number | Duplicate customers, orders that cannot be billed to a known payer, a B2B hierarchy that does not match the one the ERP prices against |
| Consent and preferences | Consent management platform, or the CDP where it holds the auditable record | CMP capture points (web, app, call centre, paper); the CDP where it is the record | Marketing automation, commerce platform, analytics tooling, service | Consent captured per channel and stored in each channel's own table; withdrawal propagated only to the channel where it happened | Messaging without a valid legal basis, no defensible audit trail, withdrawal that does not stick — a compliance finding, not a technical one |
| Order (after capture) | OMS where one exists, otherwise the ERP sales order | OMS / ERP for lifecycle transitions; the shop for the initial capture only | Commerce platform (status display), service, customer, finance, analytics | The shop continues to advance its own order status model in parallel with the ERP, with its own state machine | Two order statuses that diverge; customer service works from the wrong one; returns and partial deliveries cannot be represented in the shop model |
| Content (editorial) | CMS | Editors, campaign managers, translators, agency users | Commerce storefront, app, kiosk, newsletter, service portal | Editorial content copied per channel and then edited in each copy | Channel drift, no single publication or takedown, legal and brand corrections applied unevenly; effort scales with channels |
| Assets (binaries and their metadata) | DAM | DAM users; creative production; supplier asset feeds | PIM (assignment only), CMS, commerce, print, partners | The shop's media folder becomes the de-facto asset library, with renditions produced by hand | No rights or expiry control, no re-rendition on format change, duplicated binaries, assets that cannot be reused in another channel |
| Search index | Derived; never a master | The indexing pipeline only | Storefront search and navigation, internal tools | Attributes that exist only in the index, corrections applied to the index because the source is slow to change | The index cannot be rebuilt without data loss — the strongest form of accidental mastership, and normally discovered only when the index is rebuilt |
| Analytics and behavioural data | CDP or data warehouse / lakehouse | Event collectors, ETL and ELT pipelines, model jobs | BI, marketing audiences, merchandising, planning, finance reporting | Operational decisions read directly from the analytics store, or segments computed in the shop and never written back | Operational coupling to a batch system, segments that disagree between channels, figures that cannot be reproduced for a given date |

## 3. Integration styles

| style | when to use | when not | operational cost | typical failure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| API-led request/response (synchronous) | The caller needs a fresh answer to continue, the callee can meet a latency budget in the low hundreds of milliseconds, and the data is small and per-entity: address validation, live availability for a single item, tokenised payment authorisation | High-volume bulk transfer, anything where the callee's availability is worse than the caller's required availability, anything in a checkout path whose failure must not stop the order | Moderate: API gateway, contract and version management, timeout, retry and circuit-breaker configuration, load testing against the callee's real capacity | The callee is slow or down and the caller's user-facing path fails with it; timeouts set longer than the user's patience; retry storms amplifying an outage |
| Event-driven (publish/subscribe, streaming) | State changes must fan out to several consumers, the producer must not know its consumers, and eventual consistency is acceptable: stock change, order status change, price change, customer profile change | A consumer needs a guaranteed immediate answer; the team has no operational practice for ordering, replay, poison messages and idempotency; a single consumer that could simply be called | High: broker operation, schema registry and evolution, idempotent consumers, ordering and partitioning decisions, replay and dead-letter handling, per-consumer lag monitoring | Silent consumer lag that surfaces as stale data days later; duplicate events applied twice because a consumer is not idempotent; a schema change that breaks a consumer nobody knew existed |
| Batch and file | Large volumes with a natural cut-off and no latency requirement: catalogue full loads, price list rollover, financial postings, reporting extracts, supplier feeds | Anything a user waits for; data that changes many times per day; deltas that cannot be reconstructed from a full file | Low per interface, but the operational surface adds up: scheduling, file transfer credentials, checksums, restart and partial-failure handling, reconciliation | A missed or half-written file that nobody notices until the next business day; a full load that silently deletes what it does not contain; overnight windows that no longer fit the volume |
| iPaaS-mediated | Many systems must be connected, protocols and formats differ, and a canonical model plus central monitoring and error handling is worth the hop: ERP to shop, CRM to marketing, partner onboarding | A single high-volume, latency-sensitive path where the extra hop costs more than it saves; a landscape with two interfaces; when the platform is used as a place to hide business logic | Moderate to high: platform licence or subscription, flow lifecycle and versioning, a team that can operate and change flows, environment promotion, monitoring that the business can read | Business logic accumulating in flows until the platform is the real application and no source system owns the rule; a central point of failure without the availability to justify it; a mapping only one person understands |
| Direct database access (anti-default) | Effectively never for cross-system integration. Defensible only as a temporary, read-only, documented measure with an owner and an end date — for example a one-off migration extract from a system that is being switched off | Any write from one system into another's schema; anything meant to last; anything a vendor's upgrade may reshape | Appears near zero, which is the trap: no contract, no versioning, no monitoring, and the receiving system's upgrades become the sender's problem | The source system's next release renames a column and a business process breaks with no error that names the cause; row locks from a reporting query stalling transactional work; a hidden dependency nobody can find before a migration |

## 4. Anti-patterns

Each anti-pattern below is a hypothesis to test against the client's landscape, and the "way out" is a direction, not a plan. Symptom first, because that is what is observable.

### Multiple masters for one domain
**Symptom** — the same attribute can be edited in two systems, and there is a manual habit, a nightly reconciliation job or a rule of thumb ("the ERP wins unless marketing changed it") holding them together. Asking who owns the attribute produces two answers.
**Why it happens** — one system was too slow to change, so a second edit point was opened as an exception and never closed; or two organisational units bought their own tool.
**What it costs** — permanent reconciliation effort, a drift that surfaces as customer-visible errors, and a platform move that must first decide the mastership question the organisation avoided for years.
**Way out** — decide mastership per attribute, not per system; write the decision down as an attribute-level ownership list; make the losing system read-only for that attribute in the tool itself, not by policy.

### Point-to-point sprawl
**Symptom** — the interface count grows roughly with the square of the system count; no inventory exists, or the inventory is a spreadsheet whose last edit is old; adding a system requires touching several unrelated ones.
**Why it happens** — every project connected what it needed, on time and within its own budget. No project was funded to own the landscape.
**What it costs** — change becomes unpriceable, because the true cost of any change is the unknown number of consumers; every migration begins with an archaeology phase; failures have no central place to be seen.
**Way out** — inventory the interfaces first with volumes and consumers; introduce a canonical model for the two or three domains that are exchanged most; route new interfaces through one layer, and retire old ones only where a consumer actually moves — not as a big-bang.

### The shop as system of record
**Symptom** — product attributes, customer records, prices or order states exist authoritatively only in the commerce platform; the answer to "where does this come from?" is "it is maintained in the shop"; a platform replacement is estimated mainly as a data-reconstruction exercise.
**Why it happens** — the shop was the first system with a usable editing interface, and every gap in the surrounding landscape was closed inside it.
**What it costs** — the platform cannot be replaced without rebuilding master data management; other channels cannot be served without going through the shop; the shop's release cycle gates data changes.
**Way out** — name each domain the shop actually masters, then re-home them one at a time, starting with the domain that blocks the next business initiative. Expect the re-homing to be an organisational change with a system change attached.

### The ERP called synchronously in the checkout path
**Symptom** — checkout latency and error rate track the ERP's load; the shop is unavailable for ordering during ERP maintenance windows; conversion drops when a batch job runs in the back end.
**Why it happens** — the requirement was legitimate (a customer-specific price, a credit check, a real ATP figure) and the fastest implementation was a direct blocking call.
**What it costs** — the shop inherits the ERP's availability and its release calendar; peak campaigns are limited by ERP capacity; every ERP incident becomes a revenue incident.
**Way out** — separate what must be exact at order time from what may be indicative: cache with an explicit staleness budget, pre-compute customer price lists, degrade gracefully with a documented fallback, and move the exact check to order acceptance rather than the click. Where a synchronous call is genuinely required, give it a timeout, a fallback and a load test against the ERP's real capacity.

### Business logic hidden in the middleware
**Symptom** — a mapping flow decides discounts, splits orders, derives categories or invents identifiers; understanding a business rule requires opening the integration platform; the flow has no test the business can read.
**Why it happens** — the integration team was the only one able to deploy quickly, and a mapping is an easy place to add "just one condition".
**What it costs** — the rule is invisible to both the source and target systems' owners; it cannot be tested as business logic; the integration platform becomes unreplaceable and its upgrade becomes a business risk.
**Way out** — classify each flow as routing/mapping or business logic; move business logic into the system that owns the domain; leave the integration layer with protocol, transformation, routing and error handling only.

### A search index treated as a master
**Symptom** — attributes or corrections exist only in the index; a full reindex is feared or has not been attempted in a long time; the indexing pipeline has manual pre- or post-processing steps.
**Why it happens** — the index was the fastest place to fix a merchandising problem, and a fix that works is rarely revisited.
**What it costs** — the index cannot be rebuilt without losing data, so it cannot be upgraded, retuned or replaced; search relevance work is blocked by fear of the rebuild.
**Way out** — make "the index is rebuildable from sources at any time" a hard rule and test it on a schedule; push every index-only attribute back into the owning system; keep boosting and merchandising rules as configuration outside the index document.

### Content duplicated per channel
**Symptom** — the same campaign text lives in the shop, the app, the newsletter tool and a partner portal, each edited separately; a legal correction takes days and is applied unevenly; editors keep a checklist of places to change.
**Why it happens** — each channel arrived with its own content editing capability, and reuse required an integration nobody funded.
**What it costs** — editorial effort scales with channels, brand and legal consistency degrade, and takedown is unreliable — which is the compliance-relevant part.
**Way out** — one editorial master with channel-specific presentation, delivered by API; accept per-channel variants only as declared overrides of a shared source, never as copies.

### Customer data scattered across channel silos
**Symptom** — a customer has a different identity in the shop, the service system and the marketing tool; matching is done on email address; service agents cannot see a web order without switching systems.
**Why it happens** — channels were built as projects with their own data stores, and identity resolution was deferred.
**What it costs** — no reliable view of the customer, duplicate and contradictory communication, consent that cannot be enforced across channels, and analytics that cannot count customers.
**Way out** — introduce one identity master and a stable customer key propagated to every channel; resolve identities with an explicit matching rule and a manual-review path; separate identity from profile from account instead of merging all three into whichever system is nearest.

### Per-partner custom interfaces instead of a canonical model
**Symptom** — every supplier, marketplace, carrier or franchise partner has a bespoke interface and its own mapping; onboarding a new partner is a project with a multi-week estimate; a change to one partner's format cannot be reused.
**Why it happens** — the first partners were few and each was urgent; a canonical model felt like overhead that no single partner's business case would pay for.
**What it costs** — onboarding cost stays flat instead of falling, partner interfaces cannot be tested as a family, and the number of interfaces to migrate becomes the dominant cost of a platform move.
**Way out** — define a canonical message per exchanged domain plus a thin per-partner adapter; measure onboarding effort per partner and make it fall; migrate partners to the canonical model as their contracts or formats change anyway.

### A bespoke PIM growing inside the shop
**Symptom** — custom product attributes, custom classification, enrichment workflows, approval states and translation handling have been built into the commerce platform's back office; product editors work primarily in the shop; the ERP feed is enriched inside the shop before publication.
**Why it happens** — a PIM was postponed and the shop already had a data model and an editing interface, so each missing capability was added where it was cheapest that quarter.
**What it costs** — the enrichment capability is locked to the shop's release and upgrade cycle, other channels can only be served from the shop, and a platform replacement includes rebuilding a product information system that was never designed as one.
**Way out** — treat it as a PIM introduction, not a shop refactoring: inventory the attributes and workflows actually in use, decide which are enrichment (move) and which are commerce configuration (stay), and plan a coexistence period where the shop is a read-only consumer of the new master.

## 5. Target-architecture option patterns

Each pattern is an option, not a recommendation. The prerequisites are the part that decides whether the option is real: if a prerequisite is missing, the pattern will still be technically possible and will still fail organisationally. State the prerequisites in any deliverable that proposes the pattern.

### Introduce a PIM
**What it changes** — product structure, enrichment, media assignment, relations and publication state move out of the shop and out of spreadsheets into a dedicated master; the commerce catalogue becomes a published projection; other channels are served from the same master.
**Prerequisites** — product-data governance has a named owner with the authority to decide attribute definitions; a data-ownership decision exists per attribute and is signed off, including which attributes remain ERP-owned; an attribute inventory with counts of actually-used attributes, locales and channels exists; an agreed target classification exists (or an explicit decision to migrate the existing one unchanged); editorial capacity is planned for the enrichment backlog that the migration will expose.
**Risks** — the attribute list balloons because nothing is decided out of scope; the PIM is introduced as a pass-through with the enrichment still happening elsewhere, which adds a system and removes no work; the shop keeps its editing capability "temporarily" and mastership never actually moves; supplier data quality proves to be the real constraint.
**Sequencing** — attribute inventory and per-attribute ownership decision; classification decision; PIM for one product range end to end including publication to the shop; freeze the shop's editing capability for migrated attributes; roll out remaining ranges; only then add further channels.

### Consolidate onto one CMS
**What it changes** — editorial content has one master with channel-specific delivery; per-channel copies become presentation variants; publication, takedown and translation happen once.
**Prerequisites** — an inventory of content types and templates actually in use per channel, with page counts and last-edited dates so dead content can be dropped instead of migrated; a decision on the commerce/content boundary per component (who owns a product-detail page's blocks); editorial roles, workflow and approval defined for the merged organisation; a translation and localisation process with an owner; a decision on whether the storefront renders CMS-delivered content or embeds it.
**Risks** — a migration that carries over years of unused content and templates; the boundary with commerce-owned components left undecided, so both systems build the same block; editors losing capabilities they relied on in the old tool and quietly reverting to per-channel editing; SEO and URL continuity treated as a late task.
**Sequencing** — content inventory and cull; boundary decision per component; one channel migrated fully, including redirects and search-engine continuity; remaining channels; retire the old editing capabilities as each channel goes live, not after.

### Re-home the customer master to a CDP/CRM
**What it changes** — identity, profile and segment mastership move out of the channels; every channel receives a stable customer key; consent and preferences get one auditable record; the shop becomes a consumer plus a capture point.
**Prerequisites** — a decision on which system masters identity, which masters profile and which masters the account (the three are usually different, and in B2B the account hierarchy usually stays in the ERP); an explicit identity-matching rule with a manual-review path and an owner; a legal review of consent handling and retention, with a named data-protection contact; a duplicate assessment on the real data, not on a sample; a plan for the accounts that cannot be matched.
**Risks** — merging identities that should have stayed separate (shared mailboxes and B2B contacts are the usual cause); a channel keeping its own profile store as a cache that becomes a master again; consent migrated without its provenance, which destroys the audit trail; the CDP becoming a reporting tool that no operational process trusts.
**Sequencing** — decide the three masters; matching rule agreed and tested against real data; identity and consent first, because they carry the compliance exposure; profile and segments next; make each channel's own store read-only as it is cut over.

### Move to composable commerce
**What it changes** — one suite is replaced by several specialised services with the integration and the frontend as first-class deliverables the client owns; release cycles decouple; capability choice per domain becomes possible — and so does per-domain vendor risk.
**Prerequisites** — a platform team with in-house frontend and integration engineering capacity, sized for continuous operation and not only for the build; an integration platform or event backbone that already exists and is operated; an interface inventory with volumes and peak loads exists; a total-cost-of-ownership model over at least three to five years covering engineering, not only licences; an operating model that names who is on call for each service; a defensible answer to which capability is being decomposed and why that capability is the constraint.
**Risks** — the distributed monolith, where services are separate but must be released together; the frontend becoming the new monolith and accumulating business logic; N vendor upgrade cycles landing on one team; per-service SLAs that do not add up to the business's availability requirement; a decomposition that solves a platform problem the client does not have while leaving the data-ownership problem it does have.
**Sequencing** — establish the integration capability and the interface inventory first; decompose one capability with a real business driver and measure the effect on lead time and cost; only then decide whether to continue; keep a documented reversal option for each step while it is still cheap.

### Introduce an OMS between shop and ERP
**What it changes** — order lifecycle, sourcing, splitting, partial deliveries, returns and cross-channel fulfilment move into a dedicated system; the shop becomes a capture channel with a status projection; the ERP keeps finance, billing and the goods movement.
**Prerequisites** — an agreed order state model shared by shop, OMS and ERP, with a named owner per transition; the sourcing and allocation rules written down as business rules, not as tribal knowledge; a returns and credit process agreed with finance; interface volumes and peak order rates known; a decision on which system the customer service organisation works in.
**Risks** — three systems each advancing a partial order state, which is worse than the two it replaced; sourcing rules that cannot be expressed in the chosen product and get customised until the OMS is bespoke; returns and credits left in the ERP while fulfilment moves, splitting one process across two systems; store or warehouse staff receiving a second tool without their process being redesigned.
**Sequencing** — order state model and rule documentation first; one order type or one channel through the OMS end to end including returns; extend to remaining channels; remove the shop's own lifecycle logic only when the OMS covers the cases it handled.

### Introduce an iPaaS / integration layer
**What it changes** — interfaces get a canonical model, one place for monitoring, retries and error handling, and a lifecycle of their own; point-to-point connections are replaced as they are touched; new systems connect once rather than n times.
**Prerequisites** — an interface inventory with volumes and peak loads exists, including who consumes each interface; a canonical model agreed for at least the domains exchanged most; an integration team or a named service partner with a run responsibility, not only a build one; environment promotion and versioning defined for flows; a rule, agreed and enforced, that business logic does not enter the layer.
**Risks** — the layer becoming a business-logic dump and thereby unreplaceable; a central point of failure whose availability is below the sum of what it mediates; licence cost scaling with message volume in a way the business case did not model; the old point-to-point interfaces surviving beside the new layer, so both must be operated.
**Sequencing** — inventory and volumes; canonical model for the highest-traffic domain; route new interfaces through the layer from day one; migrate existing interfaces only when a change to them is needed anyway; report the point-to-point count as a metric so the sprawl is visible.

### Split the monolith storefront into a headless frontend
**What it changes** — presentation is decoupled from the commerce backend and released independently; the frontend becomes a separately owned and operated application with its own build, delivery, performance and accessibility responsibilities; the backend must expose a complete enough API.
**Prerequisites** — in-house frontend engineering capacity for ongoing ownership, not a one-off build by a partner; an API gap analysis against the real storefront requirements, including the awkward ones (session and cart behaviour, search and faceting, personalisation, B2B approval flows, punch-out, content composition); a decision on server-side rendering and its hosting, with search-engine and performance requirements stated as measurable targets; a content composition model agreed between CMS and commerce; a plan for the features currently implemented as backend template overrides that have no headless equivalent.
**Risks** — the API gap discovered late, so backend customisation is added to serve the frontend; business logic migrating into the frontend where it is duplicated per channel; rendering, caching and edge-delivery operations underestimated; accessibility and SEO regressions relative to a mature old storefront; two storefronts running in parallel for longer than planned, doubling editorial and testing effort.
**Sequencing** — API gap analysis against the actual requirement list; content composition decision; one journey or one country headless in production with measured performance and search-engine outcomes; expand journey by journey; decommission the old storefront per journey rather than in one cut-over.

## 6. How to use this file in an assessment

Use it as a question generator and as a gap grid, and nothing else.

As a **question generator**: walk the capability map row by row and ask which client system provides the capability, then walk the SSOT table row by row and ask, per data domain, who may write the attribute, who reads it, and what happens when two answers appear. The "classic violation" column is a prompt, not a claim — the useful output is the client's own answer and the evidence for it. The integration-styles table generates a second pass: for every interface the client names, ask which style it uses, what volume and peak it carries, what its latency budget is, and what happens when it fails. The anti-pattern list generates a third pass, always as a question about a symptom that can be observed, never as an assertion.

As a **gap grid**: put the client's systems against the capability map to see which capabilities are unowned, owned twice, or owned by a system that was never meant to hold them. Put the client's interfaces against the integration-style table to see which ones are carrying a style that does not fit their volume or latency. Put any proposed target architecture against the option patterns to check whether its prerequisites are stated, and mark each unstated prerequisite as an open risk with an owner.

Every row must be re-tested against the client's constraints before it enters a deliverable: licensing and contractual limits, hosting and data-residency rules, team size and available skills, the release and change-freeze calendar, regulatory obligations, the budget shape (capital versus ongoing), and the political ownership of each system. A pattern that is correct in general and impossible for this client is a wrong recommendation. Where the assessment cannot resolve a question, record it as an open question with the person who must answer it and by when — that is a legitimate result, and more useful than a confident row copied from here.
